# Biofilms: Distributed Survival Under Antibiotic Stress

A single free-floating bacterium exposed to an antibiotic usually dies
quickly. The same species of bacteria organized into a biofilm, a structured
colony embedded in a self-secreted matrix on a surface, can survive
antibiotic concentrations hundreds of times higher than what kills their
free-floating relatives. Part of the defense is purely physical, the matrix
slows drug penetration, but the more interesting part is behavioral: cells
deep inside a mature biofilm downregulate their metabolism into a
near-dormant state, and dormant cells are far harder for most antibiotics to
kill because those drugs target actively dividing machinery. When the
antibiotic pressure lifts, the dormant "persister" cells reactivate and the
colony regrows from a small surviving core, effectively resuming from
wherever it left off rather than starting over from a single new cell. The
colony's survival strategy is not to fight every threat directly, it is to
keep a quiet, low-cost reserve of state alive through the crisis and rebuild
from that reserve once the crisis passes.
